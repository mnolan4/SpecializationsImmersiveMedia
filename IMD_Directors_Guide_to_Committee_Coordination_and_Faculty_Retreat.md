# Director's Guide to Coordinating IMD Committee Work

## Information Sharing, Decision Flow, and Faculty Retreat Planning

**Administrative Working Guide**  
**Prepared September 2026**

## Purpose

This guide provides a practical structure for coordinating the six IMD committees, sharing work before decisions become fixed, synthesizing findings, and introducing recommendations through a faculty retreat.

It does not alter formal faculty governance or assign the Director sole authority over curricular decisions. Its purpose is to help the Director create a transparent process in which committees can work efficiently within their charges while recognizing that their recommendations are interdependent.

The operating principle is:

> **Committees may investigate independently, but consequential recommendations should become visible while they can still be changed.**

## 1. The Director's Coordinating Role

The Director does not need to author every recommendation or attend every committee meeting. The Director's primary role is to maintain the architecture of the process.

That role includes:

- confirming committee charges and boundaries;
- identifying overlaps and shared deliverables;
- sequencing decision points;
- requiring common documentation;
- ensuring that affected committees are consulted;
- resolving uncertainty about ownership;
- identifying administrative constraints early;
- preserving a transparent record of decisions and dependencies;
- creating opportunities for faculty review; and
- connecting recommendations to governance, budget, staffing, scheduling, and implementation.

The Director should guard against two equal risks:

1. **Silos:** committees develop internally coherent recommendations that conflict when combined.
2. **Process overload:** every issue is referred to all six committees, slowing work and weakening ownership.

The solution is a graduated communication structure.

## 2. Establish Three Levels of Coordination

| Level | Use When | Director's Expectation |
|---|---|---|
| **Inform** | Work remains within one committee's charge but provides useful context. | The committee posts a short update or working document. |
| **Consult** | A proposal affects another committee's assumptions, workload, resources, or recommendations. | The originating committee requests specific input before finalizing and records the response. |
| **Joint Recommendation** | An issue falls directly within multiple charges or cannot be implemented through one committee alone. | Representatives divide responsibilities, co-author the recommendation, and identify unresolved questions. |

### Issues That Automatically Require Consultation

- A new, revised, or required course
- A new prerequisite or competency assumption
- A specialization, concentration, or formal pathway
- A required portfolio milestone
- A required facility or equipment experience
- A recommendation affecting faculty workload or staffing
- A public claim about careers or professional preparation
- A proposal dependent on another committee's work

### Issues That Require a Joint Recommendation

- The AI specialization, because it is shared by Coding and Specializations
- Portfolio handoff requirements embedded across multiple required courses
- Required laboratory experiences placed into courses
- Public specialization and career language jointly grounded in curriculum and external evidence
- Any proposal for which implementation authority and curricular ownership cannot be separated

## 3. Set Up the Coordination System

### Step 1: Confirm Written Charges

Provide each committee with:

- its formal charge;
- expected deliverables;
- matters it leads;
- matters requiring consultation;
- any known deadlines; and
- the format of the final report.

Each committee should be able to state its scope in one paragraph.

### Step 2: Name One Liaison per Committee

The liaison may be the chair or another member. The liaison:

- posts updates and working documents;
- receives consultation requests;
- identifies emerging dependencies;
- brings questions back to the full committee; and
- verifies that responses reflect the committee rather than an individual opinion.

The liaison does not independently approve proposals.

### Step 3: Create a Shared Working Space

Use one location with the following structure:

1. **Committee Charges and Membership**
2. **Shared Definitions and Templates**
3. **Current-State Maps**
4. **Committee Input Briefs**
5. **Draft Proposals**
6. **Cross-Committee Reviews**
7. **Decision and Dependency Logs**
8. **Pilot Evidence**
9. **Faculty Retreat Materials**
10. **Final Recommendations**

Every document should include an owner, status, date, and next action. Drafts should be marked clearly, but visible drafts are preferable to finished surprises.

### Step 4: Require Common Documents

#### Committee Input Brief

Each committee produces a concise brief containing:

- current state;
- evidence reviewed;
- central problem or opportunity;
- working assumptions;
- constraints;
- emerging recommendations;
- decisions that may affect other committees; and
- questions requiring input.

#### Decision Log

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

#### Dependency Register

| Proposal | Depends On | Affected Committee | Input Needed By | Status | Risk if Unresolved |

#### Consultation Request

Each request should state:

- the proposal under consideration;
- why the receiving committee is affected;
- the specific question or decision requiring response;
- supporting evidence;
- the consequences of action or inaction;
- the response date; and
- the person responsible for follow-up.

## 4. Establish a Predictable Cadence

### Monthly Chair or Liaison Checkpoint

Schedule a brief meeting, ideally 30 to 45 minutes. Each committee reports:

1. **What we learned**
2. **What we are considering**
3. **What we need from another committee**

The meeting is for dependency management, not extended deliberation.

### Shared Draft Exchange

Set at least two common draft dates:

- an **early working-draft exchange**, when substantial revision is still expected; and
- a **pre-recommendation exchange**, before reports are treated as final.

Without shared dates, committees will finish at different times and early finishers may create assumptions that late-stage committees cannot realistically challenge.

### Director's Written Synthesis

After each major exchange, provide a short synthesis identifying:

- emerging agreements;
- conflicting assumptions;
- duplicated recommendations;
- unresolved dependencies;
- decisions that require joint work; and
- administrative constraints that committees should address.

The synthesis should connect the work without rewriting committee conclusions.

## 5. Sequence the Work

### Phase 1: Shared Definitions and Current-State Evidence

All committees begin by agreeing on common terms and documenting the present curriculum, resources, and practices.

The Director should discourage premature course proposals during this phase.

### Phase 2: Committee Input Briefs

Each committee presents evidence, assumptions, constraints, and questions within its charge. These briefs become the inputs for other groups.

### Phase 3: Provisional Recommendations

Committees develop proposals using the shared evidence and input briefs. Proposals should be marked as provisional and should name dependencies explicitly.

### Phase 4: Cross-Committee Impact Review

Affected committees answer:

1. What assumption does this proposal make about our work?
2. What workload, resource, timeline, or curricular effect would it create?
3. What can be implemented now?
4. What requires a pilot, formal approval, staffing, or funding?
5. What remains unresolved?

The originating committee then revises the proposal or records why a concern remains unresolved.

### Phase 5: Faculty Retreat

The retreat introduces evidence, emerging findings, dependencies, and options to the faculty before recommendations are finalized.

### Phase 6: Pilot and Formal Recommendation

Committees use retreat feedback to refine immediate actions, pilots, and longer-term proposals. Final reports distinguish what faculty have reviewed from what remains exploratory.

## 6. Director's Decision Gates

The Director can prevent closed or premature decisions by establishing five gates.

### Gate 1: Evidence

No structural proposal advances without a current-state description and evidence of the problem it is intended to solve.

### Gate 2: Dependency

No proposal advances without identifying affected committees, courses, resources, and administrative requirements.

### Gate 3: Consultation

No proposal is treated as final until affected committees have had a reasonable opportunity to respond.

### Gate 4: Faculty Review

No major curricular direction, specialization, or required-course change is presented as settled before faculty discussion through the retreat or another appropriate forum.

### Gate 5: Feasibility and Governance

No recommendation is described as ready for implementation until staffing, scheduling, budget, facilities, curriculum approval, and governance requirements have been identified.

## 7. Managing the Major Areas of Overlap

### Coding and Specializations

Coding leads technical foundations and B.A. coding needs. Specializations leads the common specialization framework, Game Design, and project continuity. AI requires a joint recommendation.

The Director should ask for one reconciled AI framework rather than two competing reports.

### Portfolio and Specializations

Specializations defines what evidence demonstrates depth. Portfolio determines how, when, and where students document and present that evidence, including the role of IMDM351.

The Director should ask the committees to co-design handoff and portfolio milestones rather than add parallel requirements.

### Labs and Curricular Committees

Curricular committees define required learning experiences. Labs evaluates access, capacity, support, safety, cost, maintenance, and sustainability.

The Director should not allow a required facility experience to be promised before Labs provides a feasibility response.

### IMDM101 and Downstream Committees

IMDM101 establishes the common introduction. Other committees may identify concepts requiring early exposure, but should not turn IMDM101 into a survey of every future specialization.

The Director should ask committees to specify the minimum early signal rather than prescribe complete assignments.

### Vision and Curricular Committees

Vision supplies career research and external evidence. Curricular committees establish what students actually learn and produce.

The Director should require public career claims to be jointly grounded in both forms of evidence.

## 8. Preparing the Faculty Retreat

### Retreat Purpose

The retreat should allow faculty to:

- understand the current-state evidence;
- see how the six committee charges intersect;
- test emerging recommendations against the student journey;
- identify dependencies and unintended consequences;
- distinguish immediate improvements from structural changes;
- establish priorities for pilots and further development; and
- understand what will happen after the retreat.

The retreat should not be framed as a vote on fully formed proposals. Faculty should encounter the work while meaningful revision remains possible.

### Materials Distributed Two Weeks Before

Provide one concise packet containing:

1. A one-page overview of the six committee charges.
2. A current-state curriculum and competency map.
3. A two-page brief from each committee.
4. A cross-committee dependency matrix.
5. Short summaries of the Game Design and AI specialization cases.
6. A list of decisions that are immediate, pilotable, structural, or unresolved.
7. Retreat questions and a short pre-retreat response form.

Avoid distributing six long reports as the primary packet. Full reports can remain available as appendices.

### Pre-Retreat Faculty Questions

Ask faculty to respond briefly to:

1. Where does the curriculum currently provide strong progression?
2. Where do students repeatedly begin again rather than build forward?
3. Which proposed direction most clearly supports student depth?
4. Which proposal creates the greatest concern about access, workload, staffing, or resources?
5. What dependency or conflict is missing from the current analysis?

The Director or facilitator should summarize responses before the retreat without attributing comments unless permission is given.

## 10. How Committees Should Present Findings

To keep the retreat focused and comparable, every committee should use the same five-part format:

1. **Current State:** What exists now?
2. **Finding:** What pattern, gap, duplication, or opportunity did the committee identify?
3. **Evidence:** What supports that finding?
4. **Emerging Recommendation:** What might the program do?
5. **Dependency or Decision Needed:** Who else is affected, and what remains unresolved?

Each statement should be labeled by status:

- **Evidence:** documented current condition
- **Interpretation:** the committee's reading of the evidence
- **Option:** one possible response
- **Emerging Recommendation:** the committee's current preference
- **Decision Needed:** matter requiring faculty, Director, administrative, or governance action

This prevents an early idea from being mistaken for an approved decision.

## 11. Retreat Activities

### Student-Journey Mapping

Assign mixed groups rather than committee groups. Give each group a student profile, such as:

- a B.A. student interested in Game Design and sound;
- a B.S. student interested in AI and interactive installation;
- a student who discovers a specialization late;
- a student carrying a promising project from IMDM290 toward Capstone; or
- a student whose intended pathway depends on equipment or an oversubscribed elective.

Ask each group to identify:

- the courses and experiences available;
- missing preparation;
- likely bottlenecks;
- project and portfolio handoffs;
- facility and equipment needs;
- career or advising messages; and
- decisions that cross committee boundaries.

### Proposal and Dependency Gallery

Use one station for each major proposal or cluster. Every station should display:

- the problem being addressed;
- the current-state evidence;
- the proposed response;
- what could be done immediately;
- what requires a pilot or formal change;
- affected committees and resources; and
- two or three focused questions.

Faculty leave comments under four headings:

- **Strengthens the proposal**
- **Concern or unintended consequence**
- **Missing evidence**
- **Connection to another committee**

### Prioritization

Do not ask faculty only to vote for favorite ideas. Use four dimensions:

| Dimension | Question |
|---|---|
| **Student Impact** | How meaningfully would this improve the student journey? |
| **Feasibility** | Can it be implemented with current or realistically obtainable capacity? |
| **Dependency** | How much unresolved work must occur elsewhere first? |
| **Evidence** | Is the case strong enough to act, or is a pilot needed? |

Place proposals into four categories:

1. **Act now**
2. **Pilot and assess**
3. **Develop further**
4. **Defer or decline**

This produces a more useful result than a simple popularity ranking.

## 12. Facilitation Norms

The Director or facilitator should state the following norms at the beginning:

- Critique proposals, not colleagues or committees.
- Distinguish evidence from preference.
- Name downstream effects rather than assuming another committee will solve them.
- Do not treat silence as agreement.
- Record minority views and unresolved concerns.
- Avoid reopening settled factual questions without new evidence.
- Do not use external industry opinion as a substitute for faculty judgment.
- Do not present administrative constraints as neutral facts without explaining their basis.
- Keep immediate improvements separate from formal curricular change.

## 13. What the Retreat Should and Should Not Decide

### Appropriate Retreat Outcomes

- Shared recognition of current-state strengths and problems
- Identification of cross-committee dependencies
- Priorities for immediate changes and pilots
- Direction for further proposal development
- Questions requiring administrative research
- Areas of broad support, concern, or disagreement
- Owners and deadlines for next steps

### Outcomes That Usually Require Later Process

- Formal course approval
- Changes to degree requirements or prerequisites
- Approval of a concentration or specialization
- Faculty hiring commitments
- Budget and equipment commitments
- Final public career claims
- Changes governed by college or university procedures

The retreat can establish direction without bypassing governance.

## 14. Post-Retreat Process

### Within 48 Hours

- Share the agenda, presentation materials, activity outputs, and unedited written feedback.
- Publish a concise record of agreements, questions, and assignments.

### Within One Week

- Each committee reviews the feedback relevant to its charge.
- Cross-committee pairs meet where a dependency or conflict was identified.
- The Director updates the decision and dependency logs.

### Within Two Weeks

- Committees submit revised recommendations or a written explanation of why no revision is proposed.
- The Director circulates a synthesis separating immediate actions, pilots, structural proposals, unresolved questions, and administrative constraints.

### Before Final Adoption

- Faculty receive the revised synthesis and can see how retreat feedback was addressed.
- Formal curricular, budgetary, staffing, and governance processes are identified.
- Public language is held until the underlying structure and resources are credible.

## 15. Resolving Disagreement or Overlap

When committees disagree:

1. Record the disagreement in neutral language.
2. Identify whether the disagreement concerns evidence, values, resources, ownership, or implementation.
3. Ask each committee to state the consequences of its preferred option.
4. Require a short joint brief presenting the alternatives, shared ground, and unresolved tradeoffs.
5. Determine whether the matter belongs with the Director, the faculty as a whole, another administrator, or formal governance.
6. Preserve the dissenting view in the final record.

The Director should not resolve a curricular disagreement administratively merely because coordination is difficult. The Director should clarify process, ownership, constraints, and the appropriate decision body.

## 16. Director's Checklist

### Before Committee Work Accelerates

- [ ] Written charges and expected deliverables are confirmed.
- [ ] Committee liaisons are named.
- [ ] Shared definitions and templates are available.
- [ ] Draft-exchange dates are scheduled.
- [ ] The decision log and dependency register are active.
- [ ] AI is established as a joint Coding and Specializations workstream.

### Before the Retreat

- [ ] Current-state evidence has been synthesized.
- [ ] Each committee has submitted a concise input brief.
- [ ] Major overlaps and unresolved dependencies are visible.
- [ ] Retreat materials label evidence, interpretation, options, and emerging recommendations.
- [ ] Faculty receive materials at least two weeks in advance.
- [ ] Activities are designed to gather usable feedback rather than only reactions.
- [ ] A note-taking and documentation plan is assigned.

### After the Retreat

- [ ] Materials and feedback are shared promptly.
- [ ] Owners and deadlines are recorded.
- [ ] Committees explain how feedback affected their recommendations.
- [ ] Immediate actions, pilots, and structural changes are separated.
- [ ] Administrative and governance pathways are identified.
- [ ] Final reports show dependencies and unresolved concerns.

## Working Conclusion

The Director's most important contribution is not choosing among isolated committee ideas. It is creating a process through which the six committees can produce recommendations that fit together.

A successful process will make current-state evidence visible, surface dependencies early, give faculty meaningful influence before proposals harden, and distinguish between actions that can begin immediately and changes that require further evidence, resources, or approval.

The faculty retreat should be the point at which the program collectively sees the system, not the point at which committees unveil finished decisions.
